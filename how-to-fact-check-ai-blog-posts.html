How to Fact-Check AI-Generated Blog Posts Before Publishing

AI Toolbox · Published October 5, 2026 · AI-assisted draft revised and reviewed by AI Toolbox

An AI draft can read smoothly while getting a date, price, statistic or quotation wrong. Before publishing, identify the factual claims, open the supporting sources and correct or remove anything you cannot substantiate.

This guide gives small business owners and beginner bloggers a practical routine for reviewing a draft. It does not assume that an AI tool has already verified its output.

Why a fluent draft still needs checking

AI tools can produce incorrect information and fabricated references. Tools that search the web can improve access to current information, but a citation still needs checking: does the source exist, and does it support the sentence?

OpenAI’s accuracy guidance describes these limitations and recommends verifying important information, including quotes, data and references.

For your website, the goal is useful, accurate content. Google’s guidance on generative AI content emphasizes accuracy, quality and relevance. It also warns that generating many pages without adding value can violate its spam policies. Using AI alone does not establish that an article is reliable.

1. Make a list of checkable claims

Read the draft and flag:

  • Numbers, percentages and survey results.
  • Names, dates and historical events.
  • Direct quotations and attributed opinions.
  • Product prices, features and plan limits.
  • Research findings and statements beginning “studies show.”
  • Recommendations that depend on a specific factual claim.

Break a sentence with several facts into separate items. A tool can help extract the list, but check whether it missed anything.

Use this prompt as an organizing aid:

List the checkable factual claims in this draft without rewriting it. Preserve the exact wording. Show any sources already supplied and mark missing sources as UNSOURCED. Do not invent references.

An AI-generated source list is a set of leads to investigate, not confirmation.

2. Trace statistics to their origin

For each number, locate the original report, dataset or study where possible. Check the publication date, population, sample, geographic scope and wording.

A survey of one industry cannot automatically support a statement about all small businesses. A forecast is not an observed result. An older figure may still be useful if you clearly identify its date.

Search results and Wikipedia can help you locate evidence. Open the underlying sources before using them as support.

If you cannot trace a statistic, remove it or replace the sentence with a claim you can support.

3. Verify quotations and attribution

Search the exact words with the speaker’s name. Look for an original recording, transcript, interview, speech or publication.

Check both the wording and context. Several websites repeating the same quotation may all be copying one unsupported attribution.

If you cannot verify a quote, remove it. Only paraphrase the underlying idea if you can independently support that idea; dropping the quotation marks or person’s name does not resolve an unsupported claim.

4. Check prices and features on official pages

Open the provider’s pricing page and documentation. Record:

  • The currency and amount.
  • Monthly versus annual billing.
  • Whether an advertised monthly equivalent requires annual payment.
  • Free-plan or trial limits.
  • Which plan includes each feature.
  • The date you checked.

If official pages conflict, resolve the difference before publishing a definitive statement. Avoid calling a limited trial “free software” without explaining the limits.

Worked example: correcting a pricing claim

The following example is fictional.

An AI draft says: “ExampleWriter costs $10 per month and includes unlimited articles.”

The fictional pricing page actually lists $120 billed annually, with a limit of 20 articles per month.

The corrected sentence is:

ExampleWriter’s annual plan costs $120 upfront, equivalent to $10 per month, and includes up to 20 articles per month.

This correction fixes two separate problems: the billing commitment and the usage limit. For a real product, add a link to its official pricing page and the date checked.

5. Record the outcome of each check

Give each claim one outcome: keep, correct or remove. Save the supporting link and a brief note about what it confirms.

Review the final article for contradictions, broken links and statements stronger than the evidence. For specialized advice with significant consequences, use qualified review appropriate to the subject rather than relying solely on a general AI draft.

If a product link earns you a commission, include a clear disclosure near the recommendation.

Pre-publish checklist

  • Every important factual claim has supporting evidence.
  • Statistics retain the original date, scope and context.
  • Quotes have verified wording and attribution.
  • Citations exist and support the associated sentences.
  • Prices, billing terms and features match official information.
  • Unsupported claims have been corrected or removed.
  • Links work and the article is internally consistent.
  • Affiliate relationships are clearly disclosed where relevant.
  • Verification notes are saved for future updates.

FAQ

Can another AI tool do the checking?

It can flag possible errors and find candidate sources. Treat those results as leads and inspect the underlying evidence yourself.

Does an AI detector verify facts?

No. Estimating how text was written does not establish whether its claims are correct.

What if I cannot verify a claim?

Remove it, narrow it to what your evidence supports, or explain the uncertainty when that uncertainty itself is useful to the reader.

Do I need to check everything again at every update?

Focus on changed claims and information likely to have become outdated. Keep the earlier verification notes so you can identify what needs a fresh check.

Make verification part of your publishing routine

Start with numbers, quotations, prices and consequential recommendations. Check each against suitable evidence, correct the wording and save your notes. Publish when the claims are supported—not merely when the draft sounds convincing.
